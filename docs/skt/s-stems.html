I.Only six cells actually move

The point of the comparison is how little changes, not how much. Of the twenty-one case-forms, fifteen are identical in the two paradigms. The difference between a neuter tapas and its masculine bahuvrīhi kṛtsna-tapāḥ is concentrated in a single quadrant of the table — the nominative and accusative in all three numbers. Everywhere else (instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, locative), neuter and masculine consonant stems use the same endings and the two paradigms collapse into one.

Neuter and masculine s-stems diverge in the “strong” cases and coincide in the “weak” ones. Memorise the four divergent rows and the rest is free.

II.Where the long ā actually appears

There is exactly one place in the whole paradigm where the stem vowel lengthens: the nominative singular, tapaḥ → °tapāḥ. This is the familiar compensatory lengthening of masculine and feminine -as stems — the same rule that turns sumanas into sumanāḥ or candramas into candramāḥ: when the final -s of the stem is lost at word-end, the preceding a stretches to ā (Whitney §418; Pāṇini 6.4.14 atvasantasya ca).

In the rest of the paradigm the stem remains tapas-, short vowel and all. The acc. sg. is °tapasam, the ins. sg. is °tapasā, the gen. pl. is °tapasām. The long ā that appears in the citation form kṛtsna-tapāḥ is a property of the nominative singular, not of the stem.

III.What the other five diff cells are doing

The acc. sg. takes the normal masculine consonant-stem ending -am (°tapasam), so the neuter homophony of nom. = acc. is broken. The dual pair tapasī (neuter) gives way to °tapasau (masculine dual -au). And the plural tapāṃsi — with its characteristic neuter infixed nasal and vṛddhi — is replaced by the plain masculine consonant-stem plural °tapasaḥ. These are the six divergent cells.

IV.The fifteen shared forms

The five grey rows (ins./dat./abl./gen./loc.) are identical across all three numbers because masculine and neuter consonant stems share the entire weak-case system: -ā, -e, -aḥ, -i in the singular; -bhyām, -soḥ, -oḥ in the dual; -bhiḥ, -bhyaḥ, -ām, -su in the plural. Where the endings are the same, there is nothing for the gender change to act on. These forms are the anchor of the paradigm.
