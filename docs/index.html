Dream-passage manuscripts: a map

14 October 2026 · Maria Iontseva

Every manuscript witness we have identified for the dream passages of the DPhil corpus, with what we know about it, where it physically sits, and how far we are from an image of it. Legend: 🟢 in hand or freely downloadable · 🟡 microfilm or scan exists, request needed · 🔴 on site only · 📖 published, manuscript not required. ⚠ = not yet checked against the catalogue or edition introduction.

Five headline topics (each is developed below and in the register):

  1. One incubation rite, twelve centuries. The svapnamāṇavaka / svapnādhipati module runs from Varāhamihira’s court (6th c.) through Piṅgalāmata (1176/77) into 27+ tantric witnesses; it gates initiation, liṅga stones, temple sites and yantras.
  2. The “Svapna-X” template. A late wave of dream deities (Svapnavārāhī, Svāpnavatī, Svapneśvarī, Svapnadurgā …) that no catalogue has seen as one phenomenon; Florence 410 and Bikaner 1342 are its unread Sanskrit witnesses.
  3. The guru inside the disciple’s dream. Tantrāloka 15 teaches the entry; ORL 2379 alone names the mechanism (svapnasaṃkrānti through the left channel) and sits in a datable Kashmiri lineage (Manodadatta, 1335/6).
  4. Lucidity, Śaiva and Buddhist. Śivasūtra, Spanda and Tantrāloka 10 and 15 describe awareness inside the dream; Nāropa’s dream yoga is a different machine; shared ground is the kriyā substrate of dream signs (MMK 15).
  5. Is a dream real? The dream as one of several objective proofs at initiation (Vārāhītantra’s scale test), and Utpaladeva’s argument that dream objects have causal efficacy (Śivadṛṣṭivṛtti 4.19–21ab, the passage John Nemec pointed to).

What we are looking for, in plain words

The object. Sanskrit tantric texts (5th–17th c.) in which the dream is used: a procedure with a stated result, not a sign that merely gets interpreted. The practitioner goes to sleep on purpose, with a mantra, on a prepared bed, and something is expected to happen. Omen books (what it means to see an elephant) are outside the project; they appear here only as controls.

Four kinds of “miracle”, by what the dream changes.

Kind What happens Typical passage
Revelation a deity (or the “little dream man”, svapnamāṇavaka) answers a question in the dream: will the rite succeed, what is past, present and future, where the treasure lies Niśvāsa Guhyasūtra 3; JY Ṣ3 oracle; Yoginītantra 7; the whole svapnādhipati family
Empowerment the dreamer’s status changes: a mantra or initiation received in a dream, a text revealed in the half-waking state Mahārthamañjarī frame story; Śaktisaṃgama’s rules for dream-obtained mantras
Magic the dream or sleep of someone else is the target: send a dream, enter a sleeper, destroy a woman’s sleep as vaśīkaraṇa, put a corpse to sleep JY Ṣ4 yakṣiṇī; Vārāhī/Parātantra/Tridaśaḍāmara; Indrajālavidyāsaṃgraha
Transformation the dream state itself is worked on: stay aware inside the dream, enter the disciple’s sleeping body through the left channel, restructure the four states Śivasūtra, Tantrāloka 10 and 15, Dīkṣāpaddhati svapnasaṃkrānti

A fifth, institutional use runs through all of them: the night before initiation the candidate sleeps and reports the dream, and the guru decides whether the dīkṣā proceeds (adhivāsana). The same test is applied to a stone before it becomes a liṅga, to a temple site, and to a yantra.

Where we looked. By formula rather than by title: svapne kathaya me tathyam, hili hili śūlapāṇaye, svapnādhipataye namaḥ, ghore svapna. Corpora: Muktabodha (479 e-texts), GRETIL, SARIT, 84000, the NGMCP catalogue (15,833 cards), Dyczkowski’s unpublished editions, and twenty-odd manuscript catalogues from Kathmandu to Florence. The result is this map.

The most interesting findings so far.

  1. One liturgical module, everywhere. The svapnamāṇavaka / svapnādhipati incubation (kuśa bed, one garment, right side, the verse namaḥ śambho trinetrāya … svapnādhipataye namaḥ, morning report) has at least 27 witnesses across Śaiva Siddhānta, Kaula, Śākta and Pāñcarātra texts. Its source is not tantric at all: Varāhamihira’s Bṛhadyātrā 16 (6th c.), a king’s dream before a campaign. From the court it passes into Piṅgalāmata (manuscript dated 1176/77), and from there into every later text. Twelve centuries of one verse.
  2. Two lines, not one. The old, male Svapnādhipati/Śambhu line and a late (17th–19th c.) wave of dream deities built on one template, mostly goddesses but also male figures — Svapnavārāhī, Svāpnavatī, Svapneśvarī, Svapnadurgā, Svapnatripurasundarī, even Svapnagaṇapati and Svapnahanumat. “Svapna-X” is a productive naming pattern, not a single cult. Nobody has described it.
  3. ORL 2379 is not an anonymous 11th-century manual: it carries Manodadatta’s author verse and is a sixth, unstudied witness of the Kashmiri Kalādīkṣāpaddhati (1335/6; expanded 1598). Its dream block contains the only attestation in the whole corpus of svapnasaṃkrānti — the guru entering the sleeping disciple through the left channel to “transfer” good and bad dreams. Tantrāloka 15 describes the entry but never names the channel.
  4. The dream is one proof among several. Vārāhītantra 2 lists five “objective proofs” of initiation: flowers that rise by themselves, water that climbs out of the conch, the candidate weighed on a scale and found lighter because his karma is cut (tulā-pratyaya), and the dream. The dream test belongs to a technology of verification, not to visionary religion.
  5. A story confirms the ritual. Kathāsaritsāgara 1.6.137–138 (Kashmir, c. 1070): Śarvavarman performs the svapnamāṇavaka rite at night and a divine kumāra opens a lotus in his dream — the “little man” of the rite’s name appears as a boy, and the dream yields a grammar (the Kātantra). Dream as a source of scriptural authority.
  6. A vernacular layer exists and is filmed: Nepali translations of Svapnaprabodha and Svapnādhyāya (NGMPP A 433), a Newari stotra against bad dreams, and a Wellcome amulet book where the Sanskrit oracle verse is interleaved with Hindavi dohās naming a sultan.
  7. Magic has sub-types nobody has sorted: sleep-induction for covert entry, dream implantation, sleep deprivation as a curse (three witnesses share one verse), forced awakening, and re-sleeping a raised corpse.

Where the Buddhists come in.

  • The earliest layer is shared, not borrowed. Mañjuśrīmūlakalpa ch. 15 sorts dreams by the watches of the night and the three humours exactly as court astrology does (Bṛhadyātrā, Atharvavedapariśiṣṭa 68): the dream-sign module is a common kriyā substrate under both Śaiva and Buddhist tantra. Goodall has already shown formulaic overlap between Niśvāsa Guhyasūtra and the MMK.
  • The initiation dream test has a Buddhist twin: Dīpaṃkarabhadra’s Guhyasamājamaṇḍalavidhi 210–213 (and Saṃvarodaya 18.22) send the candidate to sleep before the abhiṣeka and read śubhāśubhaṃ svapnam in the morning.
  • The Dīkṣāpaddhati’s Vāmana mantra (“come, come, child-formed one, tell me past, present and future”) parallels the MMK’s Mañjuśrī-as-kumāra dream mantra: duplicated imperative, juvenile deity, PHAṬ SVĀHĀ. The youthful dream deity is a cross-sectarian figure.
  • Two Tibetan dream sādhanas survive only in translation, both through Khro-phu lotsāwa c. 1200: Candramitra’s Svapnatāropāyikā (Toh 1720) and Vimalasvapnabhāvanā (Toh 2786). The Sanskrit is lost — but Florence MS 410 is a Tārā-centred Sanskrit dream text, and the Sādhanamālā has Vajratārā dream mantras: three independent witnesses to a “dream Tārā”.
  • Nāropa’s dream yoga is a different machine. The Six Dharmas aim at lucidity and transformation of the dream body (rmi lam, illusory body, clear light); the Śaiva material aims at verification and oracle. Correspondences exist (the throat as the seat of dream in both), but the direction of borrowing cannot be shown — only that the Śaiva operational texts are attested earlier. Maitrīpa’s Svapnanirukti survives in Sanskrit and is the one Buddhist treatise on the dream state we can read in the original.
  • Nepal holds 45 Tibetan dream-yoga manuals on film (Lo chen Dharmaśrī, gTer bdag gling pa, the Fifth Dalai Lama) — an unexpected resource for the comparative chapter.

Lucidity on the Śaiva side. The Kashmiri exegetes already have a vocabulary for being awake inside a dream, a thousand years before LaBerge:

  • Śivasūtra I.9–11 with Kṣemarāja: a ninefold grid of states in which svapna-suṣupti is the dreamer who knows “I am dreaming”; the yogin is to carry the fourth state (turyā) into waking, dream and sleep alike.
  • Spandakārikā, Vibhūtispanda 1–3: svapnasvātantrya, freedom within the dream, obtained by resting in Spanda at the moment of falling asleep; Varadarāja’s Śivasūtravārtika 70–72 equates it with the dawning of pratibhā.
  • Tantrāloka 10: a sixteenfold ladder of states where svapnasvapna and svapnajāgrat are named rungs (Dyczkowski 2023, vol. 6, pp. 445–446 nn. 281–282).
  • Tantrāloka 15.474–505: during the initiation night the neophyte is to stay lucid so that he can see for himself whether the signs are auspicious. Dyczkowski’s note 771 already calls this lucid dreaming, so the thesis is in print; what is ours is the operational layer (below) and the comparison.
  • Yogavāsiṣṭha: svapnatvaparijñāna, knowing the dream to be a dream, as a siddhi (flight, passing through fire unharmed) — the Sanskrit lexicon of lucidity outside tantra.
  • Ciñciṇīmatasārasamuccaya: a five-step scale of mystical experience (ānanda, udbhava, kampa, nidrā, ghūrmi) in which sleep is the fourth rung and yields ājñāsiddhi — sleep as an attainment, not a lapse.
  • Mahārthamañjarī: the text itself is received “having taken up the great state between waking and dream” (jāgarāsvapnayor madhyam), and the commentary gives a criterion for a genuine dream vision.

Breath, channel, and the guru inside the dream. Two reconstructions of the same rite now stand side by side. Dyczkowski (TAA vol. 8, n. 768) reads Tantrāloka 15 as counter-breathing: the teacher enters the disciple’s heart by exhaling while the disciple inhales, and reaches the dvādaśānta by inhaling while the disciple exhales, back and forth until the disciple wakes; no channel is named in the text, the commentary or the apparatus. ORL 2379 (lines 5822–5829 of the transcription) names it: the guru enters through the vāma nāḍī with oṃ huṃ praviśāmi namaḥ, rises to the dvādaśānta and returns to the heart — “thus the transfer of good and bad dreams is accomplished, so say the gurus”. Entry through the left channel is a general Śaiva technique (vedhadīkṣā, possession rites) here applied to sleep. The term svapnasaṃkrānti occurs nowhere else in the 309 Muktabodha texts or in the Tantrāloka with Jayaratha.

Do dream objects act? (the note from John Nemec). Somānanda, Śivadṛṣṭi 4.19–21ab, makes arthakriyā (Dharmakīrti’s causal efficacy) the criterion of reality; Utpaladeva’s vṛtti turns it on the dream: water drunk in a dream quenches the dreamer’s thirst, the satisfaction is given in self-awareness and “does not deceive”, therefore the dream object too is satyaṃ vyavahāryaṃ ca. The Buddhist test of reality is used to erase the line between dream and waking. It pairs with Tantrāloka 28.355–357 (there is no pramāṇa for the non-existence of the dream; Jayaratha: dream things are “also functional”) and gives the oracle texts their philosophical warrant: if a dream can act, a dream can tell the truth. Translation: Nemec, The Ubiquitous Śiva II (2021); the passage is not in the MPhil.

State of the art. The entire published treatment of Śaiva lucidity as a practice is Dyczkowski, The Stanzas on Vibration (1992), pp. 241–245 (svapnasvātantrya, the word “lucid dreaming”, the Nāropa parallel via Evans-Wentz; credits Lakshmanjoo in n. 357) and his two footnotes to Tantrāloka 15 (2023, vol. 8, nn. 768, 771). Skora 2018 and Goodwin 1995 use “lucid dreaming” only as a metaphor for the waking state and cite Dyczkowski for the rest. Hanneder 2009 on the Mokṣopāya never uses the word. The 2017 INDOLOGY thread “Lucid dreaming in Sanskrit?” concluded there is “precious little” outside Buddhism — without anyone mentioning Spanda, Vijñānabhairava or the Tantrāloka. The Śaiva evidence is invisible to the field; that is the gap.

New passages, ranked (none in the MPhil, none in print):

  1. A portable hypnagogic verse that crossed traditions. nidrādau jāgarasyānte yo bhāva upajāyate | taṃ bhāvaṃ bhāvayan yogī … — five witnesses: Jñānārṇavatantra 1.24 (where it defines turya), the Svātantryadīpikā (attributing it to “Vāsiṣṭha” and pairing it with Vijñānabhairava), Ānandabodhendra’s Tātparyaprakāśa on Yogavāsiṣṭha 6b.106.7, an Advaita yoga manual, a Nepalese manuscript. One line of technique, no Buddhist pedigree, travelling between Śrīvidyā, Kashmir and Vedānta.
  2. Tripurārahasya Jñānakhaṇḍa 9.94 and 17.12, 17.48 (with Tātparyadīpikā): an explicit instruction to “mark with subtle intellect [consciousness] in the middle region between sleep and waking”, and the doctrine that momentary samādhis occur in every gap between the three states. A late South Indian Śākta counterpart of VBh 75.
  3. Yogavāsiṣṭha 6b.106.7 anāgatāyāṃ nidrāyām … sa cidākāśa ucyate — the YV’s own sleep-onset verse, plus exact loci for the svapnaparijñāna passages: 3.57.32–33 (lightness and flight), 3.26.46, 6b.43.34, 6b.177.31, 6b.141.1 (unharmed in fire). The YV dossier is now citable.
  4. Kubjikāmata paṭala 31: “the yogin standing in yoganidrā sees the surface of the earth, gains divine speech and entry into another body” — yoganidrā as a siddhi-state in the Paścimāmnāya, the same milieu as Ciñciṇīmata’s five-stage scale.
  5. Acintyaviśvasādākhya (Siddhānta): 27.135–137 svapne ‘pi yogayuktasya yoganidrāprabhāvataḥ — whatever the yogin sees comes true, even in dream, by the power of yoganidrā; 33.26 dhāraṇāya jayet svapnam (second witness to Candrajñānāgama: dream conquered by dhāraṇā); 47.78–80 sleep performed as worship, “for knowers that sleep is the turīya-sandhyā”. With Sakalāgamasārasaṃgraha f. 38a (second witness to Jñānaratnāvalī’s nightly regimen) the Siddhānta “ritualised sleep” becomes a cross-textual type.
  6. Śivastotrāvalī 11.5 with Kṣemarāja: Utpaladeva asks why, “gone into dream”, he is not as devoted as when awake; Kṣemarāja glosses it as the wish for samāveśa in dream as in waking. A first-person statement of the ideal inside the Pratyabhijñā lineage.
  7. Svacchandatantra 7.257 with Uddyota: the yogin “neither wakes by day nor sleeps by night”; Kṣemarāja: “waking or sleeping, for him there is neither waking nor dream, since he is never parted from the light of turīya”. With SvT 11.83–125 (abuddha → suprabuddha) this is the scriptural source of Lakshmanjoo’s ladder and of the Tantrāloka 10 state-map.
  8. Spandapradīpikā of Utpala Vaiṣṇava on Spandakārikā 33–35: an independent commentary on the svapnasvātantrya verses with three testimonia (a Rahasyastotra svapnago ‘pi na vilaṅghayiṣyasi, a siddha line, a jyotiḥśāstra verse) that neither Kṣemarāja nor Dyczkowski used.
  9. Tantrāloka 6.183–184 with Viveka: the explicit definition of svapnasvapna (one takes the first dream for waking and the second for dream), next to the known ch. 10 ladder. Also Sāmbapañcāśikā 29 with Kṣemarāja (yoganidrā as entry into the “night of mental quiescence”) and Jñānasiddhyāgama, pañcāvasthādhikāra (tajjayaṃ yogasādhanam: the states are distinguished in order to be conquered).

Corrections. Sarvajñānottara 22 tatrasthas tu vibudhyeta is not lucidity: in all four parallels the verb means “wakes up” while the dream-image is still present, a rule for reading omens. Nidrājaya is everywhere a siddhi-sign, never a technique. Of 163 yoganidrā hits, about 130 are the goddess. Svapnaprabodha in Yoginītantra and the Puraścaryārṇava cluster is a vidyā name.

Not yet searched. Dyczkowski’s MASTERFILES (Jayadrathayāmala, Tantrasadbhāva, Śrīmatottara, Ṣaṭsāhasra, Kumārikākhaṇḍa) could not be read this time: the Drive copies are uncached and time out. Tantrarāja is font-garbled. Barazer-Billoret, “Dreams in the Śaivite Practice” (Indian Night 2009, pp. 159–172) is the one Siddhānta chapter on dreams and has not been read.

Two independent lines. Kashmir: KSTS with Kṣemarāja’s Uddyota (Śāradā MSS in ORL). Dīkṣāpaddhati ORL 2379 and the Kalādīkṣāpaddhati family

ORL 2379 carries the author verse of Manodaguru (lines 5887–91 of the e-text) and cites Śitikaṇṭha’s Gurupustikā: it is a witness of Manodadatta’s Kalādīkṣāpaddhati (1335/6, expanded by Śivasvāmin 1598), copied after c. 1400. Sanderson’s sigla (BORI only): A = BORI 157 of 1886–92 (Śāradā, 246 ff.), B = BORI 440 of 1875–76 (Kashmiri Devanāgarī, 111 ff.) in Khmers 2003–04, C = BORI 1147 of 1886–92 (Śāradā, 192 ff.; = B in Exegesis 2007). Unsigled: BORI 442 (143 ff., salutes Mukundānanda), BORI 541 (224 ff.); Srinagar ORL 1523 Adhivāsakalādīkṣā (24 ff., ORLS_0338 — the best comparandum for the dream block), ORL 810, 922, 1697, 2280-2, 2446, 2018-I. ORL 2379 is a sixth, unstudied witness. Redaction test: does it open with the Gaṇeśa + Pustakavāgīśvarī sub-paddhati (Sanderson’s two redactions)? Nobody has written on the dream block.
